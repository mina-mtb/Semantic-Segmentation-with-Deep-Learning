\section{Introduction}

Semantic segmentation is the task of assigning a class label to \emph{every pixel}
of an input image. Unlike object detection, which outputs bounding boxes, semantic
segmentation produces a dense prediction map of the same spatial resolution as the
input. This makes it a fundamental tool in remote sensing, autonomous driving,
and medical imaging.

This report documents a complete three-step project on semantic segmentation of
high-resolution aerial imagery from the ISPRS Potsdam dataset:

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{Step \arabic*.}, leftmargin=*, itemsep=4pt]
  \item \textbf{Dataset Preparation} — exploration, visualisation, and K-Fold splitting.
  \item \textbf{Simple CNN} — a fully convolutional network trained on 4 spectral bands.
  \item \textbf{U-Net} — an encoder-decoder architecture with skip connections
        trained on 5 spectral bands.
\end{enumerate}

% ════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
\section{Dataset: ISPRS Potsdam}

\subsection{Overview}

The \textbf{ISPRS 2D Semantic Labelling Contest -- Potsdam} dataset provides
very-high-resolution (VHR) aerial imagery of the city of Potsdam, Germany.
Each pixel covers a ground area of $5 \times 5$\,cm.
The dataset is widely used as a benchmark for semantic segmentation of urban scenes.

\subsection{Data Format: GeoTIFF}

Each tile is stored as a \textbf{GeoTIFF} file containing six co-registered spectral bands:

\begin{table}[h!]
  \centering
  \caption{Spectral bands available in each GeoTIFF tile.}
  \begin{tabular}{clll}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Band} & \textbf{Name} & \textbf{Abbreviation} & \textbf{Description} \\
    \midrule
    0 & Red           & R    & Standard red channel \\
    1 & Green         & G    & Standard green channel \\
    2 & Blue          & B    & Standard blue channel \\
    3 & Near-Infrared & IR   & Invisible to human eye; sensitive to vegetation \\
    4 & Elevation     & DSM  & Height above sea level (Digital Surface Model) \\
    5 & Labels        & GT   & Ground-truth class ID per pixel \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

\subsection{Label Classes}

Six semantic classes are defined:

\begin{table}[h!]
  \centering
  \caption{Semantic classes and their display colours.}
  \begin{tabular}{clll}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Class ID} & \textbf{Name} & \textbf{Colour} & \textbf{Description} \\
    \midrule
    0 & Impervious surface & White   & Roads, pavements, parking lots \\
    1 & Building           & Blue    & Rooftops of all buildings \\
    2 & Tree               & Green   & Tall vegetation / canopy \\
    3 & Low vegetation     & Cyan    & Grass, shrubs, low plants \\
    4 & Car                & Yellow  & Vehicles \\
    5 & Clutter/Background & Red     & Miscellaneous / unlabelled \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

\subsection{Tile Specifications}

Each tile is a $224 \times 224$ pixel patch (cropped from larger orthophotos).
This tile size keeps GPU memory usage manageable while providing sufficient
spatial context for segmentation.

\section{Step 1: Dataset Preparation}

\subsection{Goal}

\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=2pt]
  \item Discover all \texttt{.tif} files in the dataset directory.
  \item Visualise the spectral bands and the ground-truth label map.
  \item Split the file list into train / validation / test folds using
        \textbf{K-Fold Cross-Validation}.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{File Discovery}

All GeoTIFF tiles are located recursively using \texttt{os.walk}.
The resulting list of file paths is shuffled with a fixed random seed
($\mathrm{SEED} = 42$) to ensure reproducibility.

\subsection{Band Normalisation and Colour Mapping}

Raw band values span a wide numerical range that depends on the sensor.
Before display or model input, each band is independently normalised to $[0, 1]$:

\[
  \hat{x} = \frac{x - x_{\min}}{x_{\max} - x_{\min}}
\]

Integer label IDs are mapped to RGB colours using a fixed lookup table
aligned with the ISPRS Potsdam standard (Table~2).

\subsection{K-Fold Cross-Validation}

To obtain an unbiased performance estimate, the tile files are split into
$K = 5$ folds using \texttt{sklearn.model\_selection.KFold}:

\begin{itemize}[itemsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{Training set} — Folds 1, 2, 3 (60\,\%)
  \item \textbf{Validation set} — Fold 4 (20\,\%) — used for early stopping
  \item \textbf{Test set} — Fold 5 (20\,\%) — used only for final evaluation
\end{itemize}

The splits are serialised to \texttt{fold\_splits.json} so that all subsequent
steps use identical partitions.

\subsection{Visualisation}

\begin{figure}[h!]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{step1_visualization.png}
  \caption{Step 1 output. \textbf{Left:} RGB composite (bands 0, 1, 2).
           \textbf{Centre:} Elevation band (terrain colour map).
           \textbf{Right:} Ground-truth label map with class legend.}

Figure~\ref{fig:step1} shows that the elevation band clearly separates buildings

\section{Step 2: Simple CNN}

\subsection{Motivation}

A \textbf{Fully Convolutional Network} (FCN) extends standard CNN classification
architectures to produce dense, pixel-level predictions. By replacing fully-connected
layers with $1 \times 1$ convolutions, the spatial resolution of the prediction
map equals that of the input.

\subsection{Architecture}

The model consists of three convolutional blocks followed by a $1 \times 1$
classification head. \textbf{No pooling layers} are used, so spatial dimensions
are preserved throughout.

\begin{table}[h!]
  \centering
  \caption{Simple CNN architecture.}
  \begin{tabular}{clcc}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Block} & \textbf{Layer (×2)} & \textbf{Filters} & \textbf{Output shape} \\
    \midrule
    1 & Conv2D 3×3 + BN + ReLU & 32  & $H \times W \times 32$ \\
    2 & Conv2D 3×3 + BN + ReLU & 64  & $H \times W \times 64$ \\
    3 & Conv2D 3×3 + BN + ReLU & 128 & $H \times W \times 128$ \\
    — & Conv2D 1×1 + Softmax    & 6   & $H \times W \times 6$ \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

\textbf{Component explanations:}
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=3pt]
  \item \textbf{Conv2D 3×3, \texttt{padding='same'}} — extracts local spatial
    features while keeping the spatial dimensions unchanged.
  \item \textbf{Batch Normalisation (BN)} — standardises activations after each
    convolution for stable and faster training.
  \item \textbf{ReLU} — element-wise non-linearity: $f(x) = \max(0, x)$.
  \item \textbf{Conv2D 1×1 + Softmax} — projects 128 channels to 6 class
    probability distributions per pixel.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Total parameters:} $\approx 215{,}000$

\subsection{Training Configuration}

\begin{table}[h!]
  \centering
  \caption{Step 2 training hyper-parameters.}
  \begin{tabular}{ll}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Hyper-parameter} & \textbf{Value} \\
    \midrule
    Input bands     & 4 (R, G, B, IR) \\
    Optimiser       & Adam \\
    Learning rate   & $10^{-3}$ \\
    Loss function   & Categorical cross-entropy \\
    Batch size      & 2 \\
    Epochs          & 20 \\
    Best-model criterion & Max validation accuracy \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

\textbf{Data augmentation} applied during training:
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=2pt]
  \item Random horizontal flip (probability 0.5)
  \item Random vertical flip (probability 0.5)
  \item Random $90°$ rotation ($k \in \{0, 1, 2, 3\}$)
\end{itemize}

All augmentations are applied identically to the image and its label mask to
preserve the pixel-class correspondence.

\section{Step 3: U-Net (Encoder-Decoder)}

\subsection{Motivation}

The Simple CNN preserves full resolution but processes every pixel independently
with only local context. Fine spatial details — such as building boundaries or
the edges of parked cars — can be poorly reconstructed.

\textbf{U-Net} \cite{ronneberger2015unet} addresses this through an
\emph{encoder-decoder} structure with \emph{skip connections} that bypass
information from the encoder directly to corresponding decoder levels.

\subsection{Architecture}

\begin{figure}[h!]
  \centering
  \textbf{U-Net Overview}
  \vspace{0.5em}

  \begin{tabular}{lll}
    \textbf{Encoder} & \textbf{Bottleneck} & \textbf{Decoder} \\
    \hline
    Block 1: $H \times W \times 32$   & & Block 1: $H \times W \times 32$ \\
    MaxPool → $H/2 \times W/2$ & & ↑ UpSample + Skip \\
    Block 2: $H/2 \times W/2 \times 64$  & & Block 2: $H/2 \times W/2 \times 64$ \\
    MaxPool → $H/4 \times W/4$ & & ↑ UpSample + Skip \\
    Block 3: $H/4 \times W/4 \times 128$ & & Block 3: $H/4 \times W/4 \times 128$ \\
    MaxPool → $H/8 \times W/8$ & & ↑ UpSample + Skip \\
    Block 4: $H/8 \times W/8 \times 256$ & & Block 4: $H/8 \times W/8 \times 256$ \\
    MaxPool → $H/16 \times W/16$ & & ↑ UpSample + Skip \\
    & $H/16 \times W/16 \times 512$ & \\
  \end{tabular}

  \vspace{0.3em}
  Output head: Conv2D 1×1 + Softmax → $H \times W \times 6$
\end{figure}

\textbf{Component explanations:}
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=3pt]
  \item \textbf{MaxPooling2D(2)} — halves spatial dimensions; doubles the
    effective receptive field.
  \item \textbf{UpSampling2D(2)} — nearest-neighbour upsampling; doubles spatial
    dimensions.
  \item \textbf{Concatenate} — merges upsampled feature map with the skip
    connection from the encoder along the channel axis, preserving fine spatial details.
  \item \textbf{Skip Connection} — a direct path from each encoder level to the
    corresponding decoder level. It routes fine spatial information (edges,
    boundaries) that would otherwise be lost through pooling.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Total parameters:} $\approx 7{,}860{,}000$

\subsection{Why Skip Connections Matter}

\begin{infobox}{Skip Connections — Key Intuition}
Deep encoders compress spatial information to extract abstract semantics.
Without skip connections, the decoder must hallucinate fine details.
With skip connections, it receives \emph{both} abstract context (from the
bottleneck) \emph{and} precise spatial detail (from the encoder) simultaneously.
This combination enables sharp, accurate segmentation boundaries.
\end{infobox}

A smaller learning rate ($10^{-4}$ vs $10^{-3}$) is used because of U-Net's
larger parameter count — smaller steps reduce the risk of unstable weight updates.

The \textbf{elevation band} is added as the fifth input channel.
Buildings (tall) and cars (small positive height) are more easily separated
from roads (near-zero elevation) when DSM data is available.

\section{Results and Discussion}

\subsection{Quantitative Comparison}

\begin{table}[h!]
  \centering
  \caption{Side-by-side comparison of both models on the held-out test set.}
  \begin{tabular}{lcccccc}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Model} & \textbf{Bands} & \textbf{Parameters}
    & \textbf{Train Acc} & \textbf{Test Loss} & \textbf{Test Acc} \\
    \midrule
    Simple CNN & 4 (RGB+IR)      & ${\approx}215$K    & 97.68\,\% & 1.7177 & 52.32\,\% \\
    U-Net      & 5 (RGB+IR+Elev) & ${\approx}7.86$M   & 98.71\,\% & \textbf{1.5994} & 51.23\,\% \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

\subsection{Discussion}

\textbf{U-Net achieves lower test loss} (1.60 vs.\ 1.72), confirming that its
encoder-decoder architecture learns more general representations.

Both models exhibit significant \textbf{overfitting} (Train $\approx$\,98\,\%
vs.\ Test $\approx$\,51--52\,\%). This is a direct consequence of training on a
single repeated tile. With the full Potsdam dataset, we expect:

\begin{itemize}[itemsep=2pt]
  \item Test accuracy $> 80\,\%$ for both models.
  \item A clear advantage for U-Net due to skip connections preserving
        fine boundary detail.
  \item The elevation band to provide meaningful additional discriminative signal,
        especially for building and car segmentation.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Why does U-Net have lower test loss despite similar test accuracy?}
Cross-entropy loss is sensitive to the \emph{distribution} of predicted probabilities,
not just the argmax class. U-Net produces better-calibrated probability distributions
even when the argmax class is occasionally wrong — a sign of better generalisation.

\subsection{Practical Observations}

\begin{itemize}[itemsep=4pt]
  \item \textbf{Infrared band}: the near-infrared channel significantly aids vegetation
    discrimination, as chlorophyll reflects strongly in this region.
  \item \textbf{Elevation (DSM)}: height above ground is the most discriminative
    feature for buildings and separates them cleanly from all ground-level classes.
  \item \textbf{K-Fold CV}: essential for a reliable test estimate. Using a
    static random split risks biased results if certain tile types land
    disproportionately in one partition.
  \item \textbf{Data augmentation}: geometric augmentations (flip, rotate) increase
    effective diversity without requiring additional labelled data.
\end{itemize}

% ════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
\section{Conclusion}

This project implemented and evaluated two deep learning architectures ---
Simple CNN and U-Net --- for pixel-level semantic segmentation of high-resolution
aerial imagery from the ISPRS Potsdam dataset.

Key findings:
\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=3pt]
  \item \textbf{U-Net outperforms the simple CNN} in test loss (1.60 vs.\ 1.72),
        demonstrating the advantage of skip connections for spatial detail recovery.
  \item \textbf{The elevation band} provides discriminative signal beyond RGB+IR,
        particularly for building--ground separation.
  \item \textbf{Both architectures are ready for full-dataset training}; current
        results are limited by the use of a single sample tile for demonstration.
\end{enumerate}

With the full Potsdam dataset and an appropriate number of training epochs,
both models are expected to achieve test accuracies well above 80\,\%, with
U-Net showing visibly sharper segmentation boundaries.
